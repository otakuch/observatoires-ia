A modal popup (`#brief-modal`) opens when the user clicks "PDF preview":
- Locked-scroll backdrop with click-to-close

  - Header: eyebrow, title, hero meta, byline with date

### Fixed · build.py backslash interpretation in JS content
The build script used `re.sub` with a Python f-string as replacement,
which caused backslash sequences (e.g. `
` in JavaScript string
literals) to be interpreted at substitution time, producing invalid
JavaScript in the bundled output. Fixed by passing a `lambda` instead
of a string, which preserves backslashes literally. This bug was latent
since the project's start but only manifested with the new
`bodyLines.join("
")` introduced for the email body.

- Email body is plain text (mailto limitation), URL is clickable in most
  email clients

## [0.3.2] · 2026-05-03

### Added · Critical reading section in all 3 Dispatches

This release deepens the editorial weight of the sectoral Dispatches by
adding a "Lecture critique · trois objections au récit alarmiste" section
between LANDSCAPE and BY THE NUMBERS in each of the three published
Dispatches (banque suisse #001, cadres France #002, luxe-horlogerie #003).

The section articulates three convergent objections to the dominant
narrative of imminent massive white-collar job disappearance, each
contextualized to the Dispatch's sector:

- **Objection 01 · Big Tech headcount paradox**. Empirical observation
  that Amazon, Google and Meta employ approximately the same number of
  workers in 2026 as in 2022, despite the four largest tech firms
  committing $700+ billion in AI CapEx for the current fiscal year. R&D
  expenditures normalized against revenue have actually increased at
  Meta, Google and Amazon since 2022. Marc Andreessen and Sam Altman
  themselves acknowledge layoffs primarily reflect pandemic-era
  over-hiring, not AI substitution. Source: Washington Post May 1 2026,
  NYT Opinion May 3 2026.

- **Objection 02 · A job is a bundle of tasks tied to authority**.
  Theoretical argument from Luis Garicano (Silicon Continent, April 22
  2026) responding to Amodei (Anthropic) and Suleyman (Microsoft AI)
  predictions of 50% white-collar disappearance. Labor markets price
  jobs, not tasks. A job is a bundle tied to the exercise of authority
  (resource allocation, conflict arbitration, accountability). AI helps
  on isolated tasks without replacing the person who exercises authority.
  Task-exposure does not predict job disappearance.

- **Objection 03 · Labor as a long-run luxury**. Formal economic
  demonstration from Philip Trammell (Stanford Digital Economy Lab,
  March 27 2026). Under hierarchical consumption preferences, human
  labor becomes a high income-elasticity good. When capital becomes
  abundant, its share in consumption falls and the share of human-
  rendered labor rises. If capital and labor are gross complements,
  infinite capital accumulation does not suffice for growth. Theoretical
  formalization of the income effect (Imas & Comin) applied to AI.

### Changed · Dispatch-specific framing

Each objection is rewritten to fit the sector:
- For banque suisse #001: bundle = case analysis + advisory + risk
  arbitration + authority; long-run scenario = wealth management as
  premium human relationship
- For cadres France #002: bundle = analytical production + coordination +
  priority arbitration + authority; long-run scenario = consulting,
  training, R&D, project direction structurally rising
- For luxe-horlogerie #003: bundle = technical gesture + aesthetic
  judgment + brand signature + authority; long-run scenario = luxury as
  indirect AI beneficiary through household budget reallocation

  formalization of the durable labor share through human-intrinsic goods.

  there is at most one human per human while machines can be numerically

  at 4.4%, AI capex at $700 billion, but tech overall headcount not

## [0.2.7] · 2026-04-29

### Added · Methodological reinforcement: AI exposure scores instability

Integration of two major studies that strengthen the methodological backbone
of the cartography on the (in)stability of task-based AI exposure scores.

- **Yin, Vu, Persico · NBER w35110** (April 2026). Empirical demonstration
  that LLM-based self-assessed AI exposure measures are highly fragile.
  Three frontier models applying the same rubric on identical tasks produce
  a 3.6-fold divergence in mean exposure, with agreement as low as 57%.
  Difference-in-differences coefficients vary 2.4-fold across annotators.
  County-level estimates flip from significant negative to insignificant
  positive depending on the model. The paper formalizes this non-classical
  measurement error and warns against treating evolving LLMs as static
  instruments.
- **Gimbel, Kendall, Kulsakdinun · Yale Budget Lab** (February 19, 2026).
  Systematic comparison of 7 dominant AI exposure metrics (Eloundou OpenAI,
  Felten AIOE, Webb, Eisfeldt, Tomlinson Microsoft Copilot, etc.) across
  867 SOC 2018 occupations. Three key takeaways: metrics broadly agree;
  disagreement is about magnitude not whether an occupation is exposed;
  exposure indicates impact zones, not automation predictions. Canonical
  examples: variance 0.03 for plumbers (full consensus on low exposure)
  vs. 0.48 for computer programmers (major disagreement on magnitude).
  Open data XLSX downloadable.

### Changed · Methodology section reinforced

- **Bias block 06.2 "Limits of task-based exposure scores"** rewritten to
  cite both new studies with their precise figures (3.6-fold divergence,
  57% agreement, 2.4-fold DiD variance, variance 0.03 vs 0.48). The block
  now reads as the strongest methodological caveat in the cartography:
  exposure scores are technical capability indicators, not employment
  predictions, and must never be read from a single measure or a single
  annotator model.

## [0.2.4] · 2026-04-28

### Added · Major international research integration

Four new primary sources integrated to strengthen the methodological backbone
of the cartography:

- **Focaldata × Financial Times Workforce AI Tracker · Wave 1** (April 2026,
  n=4,119 UK+US). Monthly tracker. Key findings: 65% of workers used AI at
  least once, fewer than 1/5 daily, L-shaped adoption distribution. Aggregate
  productivity gain +3.5% (Tech +7.8%, Finance +5.1%). Training effect +37
  points daily usage but only 14% formally trained. UK managers pessimistic
  vs US optimistic on hiring expectations. ABC scenarios (Augmentation,
  Bifurcation, Crash) with Bifurcation as most likely near-term outcome.
- **Burn-Murdoch & O'Connor · FT** (26 February 2026). Methodological
  critique of seven task-based AI exposure scores. Yale Budget Lab finding
  cited: measures agree on low-exposure occupations but diverge sharply on
  the most exposed. Three factors typically missed: worker autonomy
  (Autor 2003), institutional regulation (radiology, therapy), second-order
  demand effects (Bessen).
- **Burn-Murdoch · FT** (25 April 2026). "What the AI 'jobpocalypse'
  narrative misses". Empirical demonstration via BLS historical data that
  productivity-employment relationship depends on demand elasticity, not
  AI's technical capability alone. Software and pro services: productivity
  ↑ + employment ↑. Manufacturing: productivity ↑ + employment ↓. Spreadsheets
  killed bookkeepers but created financial analysts. Bank tellers: smartphones
  killed them, not ATMs.
- **British Progress · Pedro Serôdio** (April 2026). UK labour market
  evidence synthesis. Conclusion: few signs of massive aggregate effects
  despite 54% UK SMEs using AI. Goldman Sachs and McKinsey predictions rest
  on theoretical full adoption. Anthropic Economic Index (Sep 2025, Jan 2026,
  March 2026): stable gap between predicted exposure and actual usage.
